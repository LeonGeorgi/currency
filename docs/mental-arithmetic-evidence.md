# Evidence for easier mental conversion rules

Research note, 18 October 2026. These primary studies inform a design heuristic; they do not validate the current generator or provide a universal table of seconds for doubling, dividing by eight, or calculating percentages.

## What experiments actually show

| Primary study | Participants and task | Observed result | Applicability limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Hubber, Gilmore & Cragg (2014), experiments 1–2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4037843/) | Experiment 1 recruited 35 adults from the general population, mean age 43.2; 29 remained in the analysis. Participants solved single-digit and double-digit-plus-single-digit additions using instructed retrieval, decomposition, or counting, with and without secondary tasks. | In the no-load, double-digit condition, mean participant median response times were 1,463 ms for retrieval, 3,668 ms for decomposition, and 4,214 ms for counting. Larger problems were slower; concurrent load slowed all strategies. The second experiment distinguished executive demands from visuospatial storage. | These are instructed addition strategies, not competing currency shortcuts. The timing difference cannot be assigned as a fixed cost to every decomposition. |
| [Cragg et al. (2017)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5724815/) | Analysis included 39 adult psychology undergraduates, mean age 19.6, plus two child groups. Instructed addition strategies were combined with verbal and visuospatial control/load conditions. | Working-memory load impaired speed and accuracy for all three strategies, with similar interference across strategies. This differed from some earlier findings. | Familiar fact retrieval still requires attention; “retrieval uses no working memory” is too strong. This was addition in a laboratory, not everyday estimation. |
| [Imbo, Vandierendonck & De Rammelaere (2007), author manuscript](https://users.ugent.be/~iimbo/QJEP07a.pdf) | Two experiments each used 20 first-year psychology students. They added three 3-digit numbers or four 4-digit numbers, responding column by column under different memory loads. Carry count and carry value were manipulated. | Multiple carries and larger carry values increased difficulty; executive load interacted with carrying. The paper also discusses confounding between carry features and local problem size. | A three-step expression can hide substantial digit-level work. This is direct evidence about carrying in addition, not a measured decimal-position or currency-conversion penalty. |
| [Campbell & Xue (2001), publisher-hosted paper](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/xge1302299.pdf) | 72 university students: 24 non-Asian Canadians, 24 Chinese Canadians, and 24 Chinese students educated in China. They answered simple problems across all four operations, reported strategies, and completed multistep arithmetic. | Small problems averaged 854 ms versus 1,092 ms for larger problems, pooled across groups and operations. Strategy use and retrieval efficiency differed between groups. Procedures remained common for larger subtraction/division problems in all groups. | This comparison does not establish culture as a causal manipulation. The groups and tasks cannot define a universal hierarchy for all users or arbitrary amounts. |
| [Zimmerman et al. (2016), smartphone experiment](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0168431) | 513 self-selected game users; 448 supplied demographics, ages 12–65, mean 26.1, 88% Argentinean. The reported analysis focused on single-digit addition and multiplication. | Replicated operand-size, equal-operand, and five effects. A mixed model estimated multiplication with an operand of five was 319 ms faster (SE 22 ms) than predicted from product size alone. | Motivation, practice, language, age and selection differ from a general currency-app population. This does not show that arbitrary multidigit multiplication or division by five has the same advantage. |

## Consequences for the prototype

The following are **engineering inferences**, not experimentally calibrated constants:

1. **Count the work behind a label.** “Add one eighth” normally hides division plus retention of the original amount plus addition. It must not count as one easy operation simply because its coefficient is compact. Excluding sixths and eighths from the default vocabulary follows this user's usability requirement; these studies do not specifically rank those fractions against 10%.
2. **Prefer a small familiar vocabulary.** Start with doubling, halving, decimal scaling, and a few familiar multiples. Treat the exact ordering as a hypothesis. The five effect supports allowing learned numerical structure to matter, rather than scoring only operand magnitude.
3. **Penalize retained branches and recombination.** A sequential transformation can be easier to track than computing a correction from an original number and adding it back. Count the correction's internal work. Do not claim every retrieval is effortless or every decomposition is bad.
4. **Evaluate real amounts as well as rates.** A factor has the same relative approximation error for every positive amount, but cognitive difficulty changes with digits, carries and intermediate values. Test whole-number and decimal prices, round and awkward amounts, and both conversion directions. Decimal alignment and recurring results are reasonable extra penalties, but the studies above do not calibrate them.
5. **Allow an honest accuracy trade-off.** If simple rules cannot meet a fixed error threshold, report that gap or offer a rougher rule with its error. Do not silently introduce obscure fractions to improve coverage.

## What is still missing

No study above directly compares complete conversion instructions such as “double, scale by 100, subtract 10%” against alternatives. Their reaction times include their own stimulus, strategy and response protocols; they cannot be added together into reliable predicted seconds for a new instruction.

Validate the complete rules with target users: counterbalance alternative rules for the same rates and amounts; measure answer accuracy, completion time, abandonment, and perceived ease; distinguish first use from practice. Compare a conservative vocabulary against the original generator. Report computational coverage separately from measured human usability, and fit any numerical effort weights only after collecting suitable data.
